# The Moore statement and the USP test

Two tools, one page. Use this when you write `02-market/positioning.md`.

## The Geoffrey Moore positioning statement

Moore's format forces you to name the buyer, the need, the category, the
benefit and the one point of difference, in that order. Fill every bracket. If
you cannot fill one, that is the hole in your positioning, not a bracket to
skip.

```
For        [target buyer, the exact person, not "businesses"]
who        [the need or problem they feel, in their words]
[business] is a [category the buyer already understands]
that       [the key benefit, the outcome they get]
Unlike     [the main rival or the status quo they use today]
we         [the one thing we do that the rival cannot honestly claim]
```

Worked example, trust sector:

> For trust administrators at small Jersey trust companies who lose three days a
> month to manual file checks, Ledgerline is a compliance workspace that
> produces an audit-ready client file in one click. Unlike the shared
> spreadsheet they use today, Ledgerline logs every change with a timestamp and
> a name.

Rules that keep it honest:

- The category must be one the buyer already recognises. "A compliance
  workspace" lands. "An AI-native orchestration layer" does not. If they cannot
  file you mentally in one second, you have no position.
- The "unlike" line names a real competitor or the real status quo from your
  `competitors.md`, not a strawman.
- The "we" line is the load-bearing sentence. It must be true, provable, and
  something a rival would struggle to copy this week. If a rival can say the
  same thing, it is not your position, it is table stakes.

## The 2x2 positioning map

Pick two axes that the buyer actually weighs when they choose. Not two axes that
flatter you. Get the axes from the dimensions in your `competitors.md`: the two
where buyers disagree most about what they want are usually the right pair.

Good axis pairs for regulated Jersey work:

- Price (cheap to premium) against Auditability (manual to automatic).
- Breadth (one job to whole workflow) against Local fit (off-island to
  Jersey-built).
- Speed to value (weeks to months) against Depth (light-touch to deep).

Avoid two axes that measure the same thing (price and value, say). They collapse
the players onto a diagonal and tell you nothing. Good axes spread the players
into all four corners.

Plot at least six players: your direct rivals, the adjacent tools, and the
status quo. Coordinates run 0.0 to 1.0 on each axis. You should sit in a corner
no rival occupies. If you land on top of a rival, either your axes are wrong or
your position is not yet differentiated. Fix one of those before you move on.

## The USP test: under 20 words, no rival can claim it

Your USP is one sentence a prospect could repeat to a colleague. Three tests,
all must pass:

1. **The word count.** Under 20 words. If it needs 30, you have not decided yet.
2. **The rival test.** Read it as if a competitor were saying it. If any rival
   could say the same sentence with a straight face, it is not yours. Cut until
   only you can say it.
3. **The buyer test.** Does it name a benefit the buyer in your interviews
   actually asked for? If it describes a feature you are proud of but nobody
   requested, bin it.

Strong USP: "The only audit trail trust admins never have to build by hand."
(11 words, no rival claims it, and interviewees begged for it.)

Weak USP: "We use cutting-edge AI to streamline and transform your compliance
workflows for maximum efficiency." (14 words, but every rival says it, and it
names no specific benefit. Fails the rival test and the buyer test.)
