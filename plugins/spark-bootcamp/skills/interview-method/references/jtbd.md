# Jobs to be done

People do not buy products. They hire them to make progress in a situation they are stuck in. Nobody wants a quarter-inch drill; they want a quarter-inch hole, and really they want the shelf up so the room feels finished. That progress is the job. Your product is one candidate for the hire. Understand the job and you understand the buyer. Understand only your product and you understand nothing that matters.

This framing, jobs to be done, changes how you interview. Instead of asking about your solution, you dig into the job: what triggered it, what they hired instead, and whether the hire worked. It keeps the conversation on their side of the table.

## The three parts of a job
Every job has three moving parts. Listen for all three.

The **situation**: the context that sets the job off. "When I am closing the month-end accounts and a client query lands at the same time." The situation is when and where, not who.

The **motivation**: the progress they want. "I want to answer the client fast without dropping the close." This is the pull, the thing they are reaching for.

The **outcome**: what "done well" looks like to them. "So I look on top of it and nothing slips." This is how they judge success, and it is often emotional or social, not just functional.

## The job story format
Write what you learn as a job story. It forces all three parts into the open.

> When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].

Example, trust sector: "When a new client sends me a pile of documents to onboard, I want to check them against our requirements quickly, so I can start the relationship without spending a day on manual checks."

A good interview gives you three or four of these in the founder's own customer's words. If you cannot fill in all three blanks, you have not understood the job yet. Keep asking.

## JTBD interview questions
Lift these straight into a script. They are all past-tense and specific, so they hold to the Mom Test.

On the trigger:
- "Take me back to the last time you needed to do this. What set it off?"
- "What were you doing right before you started?"

On the current hire:
- "What did you reach for? Walk me through it, step by step."
- "What else did you consider, even the clumsy options like a spreadsheet or just asking a colleague?"

On whether it worked:
- "Where did that fall down?"
- "What did you do when it did not work?"
- "How long did the whole thing take?"

On the outcome that matters:
- "When it goes well, what does good look like for you?"
- "Who notices if you get this right, or wrong?"

## Why this beats a feature checklist
A feature list asks "do you want X, Y, Z?" and gets you polite yeses. A job story asks "what were you actually trying to achieve, and did anything help?" and gets you a map of where the pain is and what a real alternative already costs. That map tells the founder what to build first, on any of the three paths: the software slice, the hardware prototype, or the services package. Build for the job, not for the feature request.

## The one line to remember
Find the job the customer is trying to get done, and how they judge it done well. The product is just the hire.
