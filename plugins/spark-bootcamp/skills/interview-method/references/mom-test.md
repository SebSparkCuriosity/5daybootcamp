# The Mom Test

The idea is simple. Ask questions that even your mum could not lie to you about. Your mum loves you, so if you ask "do you like my idea?" she will say yes. That answer is worthless. The trick is to ask about her life and her past instead, where the facts are the facts and warmth cannot bend them. Do that with a stranger and you get the truth. Do the opposite and you get comfort dressed up as data.

The name comes from Rob Fitzpatrick's book of the same name. You do not need the book. You need these three rules.

## Rule 1: Talk about their life, not your idea
The moment you describe your idea, the conversation is contaminated. The other person now wants to be kind, or wants to sound clever, and both ruin the data. Keep your idea in your pocket. Ask about their work, their week, the last time the problem bit them. Let them talk about themselves. You learn far more from ten minutes on their life than from ten minutes on your pitch.

If they ask "so what are you building?", say you are still figuring it out and turn it back to them. You can pitch later, on Day 5, when you are actually selling. In discovery, pitching is a mistake.

## Rule 2: Ask about specifics in the past, not generics about the future
People are hopeless at predicting their own future behaviour and brilliant at describing what they actually did. So never ask what they "would" do. Ask what they did.

The future tense is a lie detector that always reads clear. "Would you use a tool that did X?" gets you a yes from almost everyone and tells you nothing. "When did you last try to solve X, and what did you do?" gets you a story, and stories contain facts: what they tried, what it cost, whether they cared enough to act.

## Rule 3: Talk less, listen more
You are there to learn, not to be heard. If you are talking more than a third of the time, you are pitching, leading, or filling silence you should have left open. Ask a question, then be quiet. The best material comes after the pause, when they carry on because you did not.

## Questions that break the rules, and their fixes

| Bad question (leading or hypothetical) | Fixed question (specific, past, neutral) |
|---|---|
| "Do you think this is a good idea?" | "Tell me about the last time this came up. What did you do?" |
| "Would you pay for a tool that did this?" | "What are you using today, and what does it cost you?" |
| "Would you buy it?" | "How are you solving this right now?" |
| "How much would you pay?" | "What is the current problem costing you, in time or money?" |
| "Do you struggle with X?" | "Walk me through your process for X." |
| "Would this feature be useful?" | "When did you last wish you had something like that?" |

## How to steer away from pitching
You will itch to pitch. Resist it. Three moves help.

First, prepare three questions before the call that are all about their world, so you have somewhere to go when the urge hits. Second, when you catch yourself explaining your idea, stop mid-sentence and ask "but tell me how you handle it today". Third, remember the goal: you are not trying to make them like the idea, you are trying to find out whether the problem is real and painful. A problem that is real and painful sells itself later. A problem you had to talk them into does not.

## The one line to remember
Opinions are worthless. Facts about the past are gold. Ask only questions that cannot be flattered.
